Step 2

Capacity and load

Central to your role as an advocate is understanding your child's disability in terms of their overall capacity. Everything else in a good plan follows from it.

1

Capacity

By capacity we mean what your child has to work with: their ability to achieve learning outcomes that reflect their abilities, to participate, and to be independent. It is finite, and it changes from day to day and over months.

Those three parts come from the law. When a school decides whether an adjustment is reasonable, the Disability Standards for Education 2005 (section 3.4) say it should take into account:

"(c) the effect of the proposed adjustment on the student, including the effect on the student's:
(i) ability to achieve learning outcomes; and
(ii) ability to participate in courses or programs; and
(iii) independence"

Capacity is what those three share. Whatever the school day uses up is not available for learning, taking part, or managing independently.

2

What changes capacity

Three things shape a child's capacity on a given day.

  • The baseline: your child's disability and their strengths. This changes slowly.
  • Load: what the day uses up, on top of the learning itself.
  • Supports: what sustains or restores capacity.

Examples only. Every child is different, and many children have more than one of these.

"Load" and "support" are our terms. Under the law, any adjustment that is reasonable and needed is required, whether it removes a barrier or adds a support.

A plan has to look at both. Removing something can look like reducing load when it actually removes a support, such as taking a child out of the sport they play with their friends.

None of this is the same for every child. What is load for one child may not be for another with the same diagnosis, and the same support can help one child and cost another. It also changes over time for the same child.

Therapy is an example. Occupational therapy, speech therapy and exercise physiology are meant as supports. For a child who is already over capacity, each appointment can be one more demand on the week, and work as load instead. The only way to know which it is, is to watch how the child is going.

3

Invisible load

People can usually see a physical barrier, such as stairs for a child who uses a wheelchair. What they rarely see, or understand, is what a barrier costs the child. That is true for every disability.

For neurodivergent children, many of the barriers are also hard to see: masking, sensory overload, boredom, the effort of reading. So the barrier and its cost both go unseen, and they are rarely adjusted for.

"Neurodivergent" here includes autism, ADHD, dyslexia and other learning disorders, and dyspraxia. You do not need the label for the law to apply. The Disability Discrimination Act's definition of disability includes "a disorder or malfunction that results in the person learning differently from a person without the disorder or malfunction".

4

How load compounds

Because capacity is finite, load in one place leaves less for everything else. One example:

  1. Boredom uses up capacity.
  2. Too little is left to manage stimming, or the social demands of the playground.
  3. The child stims openly, or withdraws.
  4. Peers notice. Stigma follows, and then exclusion.
  5. Exclusion adds load: more masking, more anxiety, less belonging.

About the terms. "Capacity" is our term for a child's capacity on a given day. The NDIS uses "functional capacity" for a person's enduring ability in communication, social interaction, learning, self-care, self-management and mobility, to decide eligibility (NDIS Act 2013, section 24). The idea is the same; the NDIS measures the long-term baseline. The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (2001) separates capacity, what a person can do in a neutral environment, from performance, what they do in their real environment. The gap between the two is caused by the environment, which is where load and supports sit.

About the law. Disability Standards for Education 2005, section 3.4(2)(c), quoted above. Disability Discrimination Act 1992, section 4, definition of disability. Check both against the Federal Register of Legislation before relying on them.

About the evidence. Autistic burnout: Raymaker et al., Autism in Adulthood, 2020, 2(2):132–143. The cost of masking: Hull et al., Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 2017, 47(8):2519–2534. Stimming: Kapp et al., Autism, 2019, 23(7):1782–1792. Boredom as an effortful, unpleasant state: Eastwood et al., Perspectives on Psychological Science, 2012. Neurodivergence as including dyslexia, dyspraxia, ADHD and autism: Doyle, British Medical Bulletin, 2020. The grouping into kinds of load and support, and the compounding example, are ours. Research on cumulative load is strongest for autism and mostly with adults; for ADHD and dyslexia the individual demands are well documented, but cumulative daily load has been studied less.
