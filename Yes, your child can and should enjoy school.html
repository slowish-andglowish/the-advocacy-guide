Neurodivergent children, and children with common learning disabilities, still experience anxiety, failure and exclusion at school in large numbers. It does not have to be that way. If your child is autistic, has ADHD, or has a learning disorder, their school has a legal duty to adjust how it teaches them so that school works for them. Most parents are never told this.

This guide explains what the school must do, what good support looks like, and how to ask for it. Written by a parent, for parents, at NSW schools. General information, not legal advice. November 2026.

2Make disability the central lens for understanding your child's experience

Expect a medical-model school to treat attendance, behaviour, mental health, marks and friendships as separate matters, each with its own department, form and person. Expect the adjustments to be minimal, reactive, dependent on the child asking, and visible to peers. Expect nobody to say the word disability.

Do not take the year in those pieces. Put the disability at the centre and read every incident through it. Ask two questions first, every time: were the adjustments adequate, and was I consulted?

Four things to expect, and what to do with them

Expect the neuroaffirming approach as the defaultThe Standards require the school to consult and adjust. That is the legal minimum, not a favour. A school that handles each incident as its own category, with no disability in it, has not met it.
Expect the school to anticipateThe duty is forward-looking. Once the school knows of the disability, it must look at the environment as it is, ask where it will present a barrier to this child, and remove the barrier before the child meets it. Not after.
Read the symptomsWhen adjustments, supports, subject design and delivery are not sufficient or correct, attendance, engagement, performance, behaviour and health are symptoms. Use them to prompt review and adjustment. They are not isolated events unrelated to the disability.
Know you are in the ActIt protects the associates of a person with disability. You are inside the law, not outside it.

Where the discrimination happens: in how each subject is designed

The Standards sit at the heart of learning: how each subject is designed and delivered. A course that does not anticipate the child's profile means the child cannot access or interact with the material as easily as their peers, and it costs them more to try. That is the discrimination. What follows records it: disengagement, an assessment the child cannot use to show what they know, a mark that reads as a measure of the child when it is more likely a measure of the barrier, and a child who internalises all of this as their own failure and limitation. Schools can anticipate: one in four students receives an adjustment for disability each year.

  1. Subject designed without the profile
  2. Child cannot access the material as easily as peers, and it costs them moreThat is the discrimination
  3. Cannot show what they know
  4. A mark that looks like ability, but records the barrier
  5. Child internalises it as their own failure

Not every child will get high marks in everything. The point is to read a mark against the design of the subject before reading it as ability.

Before you answer any school communication about any one thing, ask:

  • Is there a written plan, and do I have the current copy? If not, that is my first request.
  • Could this be related to my child's disability? Assume yes until shown otherwise.
  • What adjustment should have been in place for this situation? Was it?
  • Does the school's communication mention the disability or the plan? If not, my reply will.
  • Was I consulted about the adjustments that apply here, and when were they last reviewed?
  • Who holds the plan, and are they copied?
  • What do I want on the record: a question, a request, or a correction?

Reply to the disability, not to the incident.

3How it feels, and what that tells you

Count your confusion as evidence. A school with the neuroaffirming approach embedded creates minimal friction. You, your child and the school are in a collaborative process, curious and experimental together about how to design your child's interaction with the physical, learning and social environment so that their education goes well. If you cannot describe that process for your child's school, it is not because you missed something. It is because it is not there. Your confusion is information about the school, not about you.

If you feel confused, dispirited, worn down, as though you and your child are not welcome and the fault is somehow yours: that is the experience of discrimination. It is the experience the Act and the Standards were written to prevent.

4Your child's school has a legal duty to support them

  • Autism, ADHD and dyslexia count as disability under the Disability Discrimination Act 1992. Every NSW school must comply, private schools included.
  • The Disability Standards for Education 2005 set out what the school must do. Once the school knows about your child's disability, it must ask, decide, change and review.
  • This is the school's legal duty. It applies whether or not you make a request, and whether or not you mention the law.
Askconsult you and your child
Decidewhat is reasonable
Changeteaching, assessment, setting
Reviewthen start again

The minimum legal duty. These four steps repeat, and good support goes further.

  • The school decides first what is reasonable, after consulting you and your child. It must weigh your child's disability, your views, the effect on your child's learning, participation and independence, the effect on others, and the costs and benefits.
  • A clinician's written recommendation is direct evidence on your child's disability and the effect on their learning. It does not make a change reasonable on its own. A school might cite grounds for refusing it, such as the effect on other students, the cost, or an essential requirement of the course.
  • If you disagree with the school's decision, you can complain to the Australian Human Rights Commission, and after that go to the Federal Court.

The law sets the minimum and shows what the school must take seriously. What your child actually gets mostly comes from working with the school and persuading it, using the evidence. A school that can see you know your child's rights, and are prepared to act on them, has good reason to take your requests seriously.

Most children with neurodivergence and learning disabilities can thrive in mainstream schooling with this approach in place (Hehir et al. 2016, 280 studies; Disability Royal Commission 2023). The Standards exist to make this happen, in every school, in every sector. If it is not happening, get clear on what the Standards require, put it in writing, advocate. Stay open to other schooling options as a solution, but choose them from strength, once the school has met its obligations, not because it hasn't.

5Collaborative practice: what good support looks like

This is the aim of working together. It goes beyond the legal minimum, and your child's own view is part of every step.

  1. A
    Assemble the clinical team

    You need a diagnosis, and treating clinicians who know your child over time. They put their recommendations to the school in writing, and can advise you on which adjustments are reasonable and effective for your child.

  2. B
    Get a full assessment

    A cognitive and academic assessment shows how your child thinks and learns: strengths, gaps, processing speed, working memory and literacy. It is the reference point for every adjustment. Without it, adjustments are guesswork.

  3. C
    Find the barriers

    Check four areas: physical environment, schoolwork, communication, and social and emotional. Check each subject and setting.

  4. D
    Plan

    The school writes the plan with you and your child, and gives you a copy. It sets out what changes, who does it, and by when.

  5. E
    Look ahead

    Before each term, go through new subjects, assessments, camps, excursions and timetable changes. Plan for each one before it happens.

  6. F
    Build the child's understanding

    Teach them about their neurotype, their strengths and their tools.

  7. G
    Review

    On set dates, after any incident, and when anything changes. Then start again.

  8. ↻
    Then start again

    The journey repeats every term, after every incident, and whenever anything changes.

The Standards support steps E and F. Camps, excursions and other activities must be designed to include your child, and teaching can include building disability-specific skills.

6A diagnosis is where the method starts, not where it ends

A diagnosis predicts some of what your child will find hard. It does not say what this child will find hard in this classroom, this year. That comes from building a profile: the full assessment, read line by line against the school day, so that each score predicts a kind of task that will cost more and each prediction becomes an adjustment you ask for before the task, not an explanation afterwards. No school does this translation for you. It is the core of advocacy.

The profile is never finished. Children change by definition, and the school should be adding to it: an attuned teacher sees things in the classroom that no assessment can. When your child meets a barrier that neither you nor the school predicted or understands, the profile is incomplete, not the child. Go back for more: a language assessment, an executive function profile, a sensory profile, an occupational therapy view. Each one turns a mystery into a prediction.

Your child's assessment already predicts where school will be hard. Use it to ask before the task, not explain after.

7Children are exquisite experts on their own experience

Children show you when school is not working, often through behaviour before they have the words. Watch for:

  • Morning reluctance, stomach aches, or slowing down before school.
  • Avoiding certain lessons, places, people or times of day.
  • Exhaustion or shutdown after school.
  • "I'm no good at this" or "I'm not smart".
  • Changes in energy, mood, posture, sleep and appetite. Compare school weeks with holidays.

Ask in easy ways: in the car, side by side, in writing or in pictures. Write down their words, with the date.

9Bullying and exclusion

  • Bullying is common. Across 17 studies, 44% of autistic students were bullied at school (Maïano et al. 2016).
  • Signs include reluctance, avoiding certain places or times, and not wanting to talk about school.
  • Report each incident in writing, with the date. Ask what the school did, and ask for it to be recorded.
  • The school must have strategies to prevent disability harassment. There is no hardship exception to this duty.
  • Before any discipline, the school must ask whether the behaviour relates to the disability and whether adjustments were in place. Being asked to keep your child home "until it's sorted" is a suspension without the paperwork. Ask for it in writing.

10Attendance: think about load

  • Reluctance is an early warning. Act then, before it becomes refusal.
  • Ask: what is the load, and at what point in the day?
  • Rest restores capacity. Pressure to return before the load is reduced works against the child.
  • Exclusion, being sent home and suspension also keep a child out of school. Children treated for ADHD are excluded about six times as often as their peers (Fleming et al. 2017).

The largest load is usually the lesson itself. A subject designed and delivered without regard to your child's profile taxes them more to engage with the material than it does other students. It can be exhausting. It can also be hidden by high ability in other areas, which is why a full cognitive and academic assessment is such a useful tool: it shows where the tax is being paid.

12If it still doesn't change

  1. Write to the teacher or the person who holds the plan.
  2. Use the school's complaints policy, then go to the principal.
  3. For disability discrimination, contact the Australian Human Rights Commission, within 24 months.
  4. A disability advocacy service can help at any step.
  5. The school cannot treat you or your child worse for raising your rights or complaining. That is unlawful too.
  6. Private schools are exempt under NSW law but not under the federal Act and Standards. Complaints go to the Australian Human Rights Commission.

Words worth knowing

  • Reasonable adjustment: a change the school makes so your child can learn on the same basis as others.
  • Load: the total demand of the school day on your child (sensory, social, masking, organising, reading and writing, emotional), set against the capacity they have that day.
  • NCCD: a yearly count of students receiving adjustments. It affects the school's funding, and you can ask your child's level.
  • Cognitive and academic assessment: a psychologist's measure of how your child thinks and learns (for example the WISC-V for cognition and the WIAT-III for academic skills). It shows strengths, gaps, processing speed, working memory and literacy, and it reveals a weakness that high ability elsewhere can hide. It is the reference point for every adjustment and the evidence the school is obliged to act on.

The key laws

  • Disability Discrimination Act 1992: disability discrimination in education is unlawful, at every school.
  • Disability Standards for Education 2005: what the school must do to consult, adjust, review and prevent harassment.
  • Education Act 1990 (NSW): attendance, and the rules independent schools must meet to stay registered.
  • NSW Child Safe Standards: children take part in decisions that affect them.
  • Making a discrimination complaint (Legal Aid NSW): time limits and where to go.
