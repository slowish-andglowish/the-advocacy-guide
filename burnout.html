Step 3

Burnout and masking

How to tell when load has gone past capacity, what happens when it stays there for months, and why home and school can see such different children.

1

Signs of overload, and sources of load

It helps to keep two lists apart.

Sources of loadWhat adds to the load: reading, noise, masking, teasing, a hard subject, a change of routine, conflict at home.
Signs of overloadWhat shows when load has gone past capacity: dread at the school gate, exhaustion, long sleeps, retreating to screens, leaving, hiding somewhere small.

A sign is not the problem to fix. Pushing a child past the gate, or taking away the screen, treats the sign. Reducing the sources treats the problem.

Screens are a common example. For a child who is over capacity, a screen can be the one place with no demands. That makes it hard to give up, and easy to mistake for defiance. Limits on screens still matter, especially at night, but they work better alongside less load during the day.

2

Burnout

When the load goes past capacity again and again, over weeks and months, capacity itself shrinks. The same day that was manageable in February becomes impossible by June. Autistic people call this burnout.

In burnout, capacity has almost gone. It no longer covers school, and it may not cover looking after themselves: getting up, eating, washing. A child in burnout may spend most of the day in bed. Skills they had before can disappear for a time. A weekend's rest does not restore it. Recovery usually takes months, and it starts with removing load.

A child in burnout is not refusing school. Their capacity has shrunk below what an ordinary day requires.
3

Masking

Masking is the load of being required to fit in: suppressing stimming, forcing eye contact, hiding what is hard. It is tiring, and it is invisible to the people it is done for. Autistic adults describe stimming as a way of regulating and coping, and describe being made to stop as harmful. Most stimming affects no one else. Where it does, for example a loud stim in a room with a child who is sensitive to sound, the answer is to adjust the environment, not to stop the child.

Removing the requirement to mask works better than teaching a child to mask more.
4

Who sees what

The school sees capacity at its bestA child who is holding it together. Quiet, compliant, coping.
Parents see what it costsThe collapse after school, the long sleeps, the mornings the child cannot start.

A large gap between school and home is not a sign that the child is fine at school. It is often the clearest sign of how much school is taking. Both views are needed to design a plan and to tell whether it is working.

5

What a plan does

A good plan keeps the load within your child's capacity, while your child stays included in learning.

  • It starts from your child's capacity: how their disability affects them day to day, and what keeps them coming to school.
  • It reduces load, one kind at a time.
  • It protects and adds supports.
  • It is reviewed, because capacity changes. A plan that worked in February may not fit in June.

About the terms. "Capacity" is our term for a child's capacity on a given day. The NDIS uses "functional capacity" for a person's enduring ability in communication, social interaction, learning, self-care, self-management and mobility, to decide eligibility (NDIS Act 2013, section 24). The idea is the same; the NDIS measures the long-term baseline. The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (2001) separates capacity, what a person can do in a neutral environment, from performance, what they do in their real environment. The gap between the two is caused by the environment, which is where load and supports sit.

About the law. Disability Standards for Education 2005, section 3.4(2)(c), quoted on the Capacity and load page. Disability Discrimination Act 1992, section 4, definition of disability. Check both against the Federal Register of Legislation before relying on them.

About the evidence. Autistic burnout: Raymaker et al., Autism in Adulthood, 2020, 2(2):132–143. The cost of masking: Hull et al., Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 2017, 47(8):2519–2534. Stimming: Kapp et al., Autism, 2019, 23(7):1782–1792. Boredom as an effortful, unpleasant state: Eastwood et al., Perspectives on Psychological Science, 2012. Neurodivergence as including dyslexia, dyspraxia, ADHD and autism: Doyle, British Medical Bulletin, 2020. The grouping into kinds of load and support, and the compounding example, are ours. Research on cumulative load is strongest for autism and mostly with adults; for ADHD and dyslexia the individual demands are well documented, but cumulative daily load has been studied less.
