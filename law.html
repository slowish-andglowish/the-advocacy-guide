Step 5

What the school must do

Autism, ADHD and learning disorders are disabilities under Australian law. Your child's school has a legal duty to adjust how it teaches them. It is the law, not a favour, and it applies whether or not you ask.

1

The duty

Every NSW school, private schools included, must consult you, decide, change its teaching and review. It applies once the school knows about, or should reasonably suspect, your child's disability.

  • Autism, ADHD and dyslexia count as disability under the Disability Discrimination Act 1992. Every school must comply.
  • The Disability Standards for Education 2005 set out what the school must do: ask, decide, change and review.
  • This is the school's duty. It applies whether or not you make a request, and whether or not you mention the law.
Askconsult you and your child
Decidewhat is reasonable
Changeteaching, assessment, setting
Reviewthen start again

The minimum legal duty. These four steps repeat, and good support goes further.

The law sets the minimum and shows what the school must take seriously. What your child actually gets mostly comes from working with the school and persuading it, using the evidence. A school that can see you know your child's rights, and are prepared to act on them, has good reason to take your requests seriously.

2

You do not need a diagnosis

The duty does not wait for a diagnosis. The law covers a disability the school knows about or suspects, and it covers learning disorders.

  • The Act's definition of disability includes "a disorder or malfunction that results in the person learning differently from a person without the disorder or malfunction". Dyslexia and other learning disorders are disabilities under the Act.
  • The definition also covers a disability that exists now, existed in the past, may exist in the future, or is presumed by others (section 4).
  • The government's own guide to the Standards gives the example of a parent told the school "need a diagnosis to make changes". It answers that schools do not need a diagnosis: if they think a child has a disability, they should be making changes.
  • Many children are not diagnosed until a parent recognises the signs and pushes for assessment, often years later. A school that waits for a diagnosis is relying on the family's knowledge, persistence and money.
3

What the Standards cover

Five areas of school life, plus two duties that run through all of them: consult the family, and make reasonable adjustments.

AreaWhat it means in practice
Enrolment (Part 4)Your child can enrol on the same basis as other children, with adjustments.
Participation (Part 5)Your child can take part in everything the school offers, including sport, camps and excursions.
Curriculum (Part 6)How subjects are designed, taught and assessed is adjusted so your child can learn and show what they have learned.
Student support services (Part 7)Your child can use the school's specialist support, such as counselling and learning support.
Harassment and victimisation (Part 8)The school must have strategies to prevent harassment and victimisation, and act on them.
Consultation and reasonable adjustment (Part 3)In every area above, the school must consult you and your child, and make adjustments that are reasonable.
4

How the school decides what is reasonable

The school must weigh your child's disability, your views, and the effect on your child's learning, participation and independence, against the effect on others and the costs and benefits.

An adjustment is reasonable if it balances the interests of everyone affected. In deciding, the school should take into account your child's disability, the views of your child and family, and:

"(c) the effect of the proposed adjustment on the student, including the effect on the student's:
(i) ability to achieve learning outcomes; and
(ii) ability to participate in courses or programs; and
(iii) independence"

Disability Standards for Education 2005, section 3.4.

It also weighs the effect on others, including staff and other students, and the costs and benefits of the adjustment.

  • Those three effects together are what we call your child's capacity. An adjustment can only be judged against an understanding of how your child's disability affects them day to day.
  • A clinician's written recommendation is direct evidence about your child's disability. It does not make a change reasonable on its own. A school might cite grounds for refusing it, such as the effect on other students, the cost, or an essential requirement of the course.
  • Any adjustment that is reasonable and needed is required, whether it removes a barrier or adds a support.

Know your rights: free resources

Plain-language guides to the Standards, made with students and families. Schools are not required to give them to you, so here they are.

  • Australian Government: Disability Standards for Education resources, including Explaining the Disability Standards for Education. Available in Easy Read, Auslan and eight community languages.
  • Children and Young People with Disability Australia: getting started.
  • Children and Young People with Disability Australia: how to advocate, with workbooks from early childhood to TAFE and university.

The 2020 national review of the Standards heard parents ask for this information to be given out at enrolment. It recommended government resources instead, and did not require schools to hand them out.

6

If you disagree

You can complain to the Australian Human Rights Commission within 24 months, and after that go to the Federal Court. The school cannot treat you or your child worse for raising your rights.

  • Private schools are exempt under NSW discrimination law, but not under the federal Act and Standards. Complaints go to the Australian Human Rights Commission.
  • Treating you or your child worse for raising your rights or complaining is victimisation, and it is unlawful too.
  • A disability advocacy service can help at any step. See The advocate's role for the steps in order.
7

The key laws and words worth knowing

The laws that apply, with links, and the terms schools use.

The key laws

  • Disability Discrimination Act 1992: disability discrimination in education is unlawful, at every school.
  • Disability Standards for Education 2005: what the school must do to consult, adjust, review and prevent harassment.
  • Education Act 1990 (NSW): attendance, and the rules independent schools must meet to stay registered.
  • NSW Child Safe Standards: children take part in decisions that affect them.
  • Making a discrimination complaint (Legal Aid NSW): time limits and where to go.

Words worth knowing

  • Reasonable adjustment: a change the school makes so your child can learn on the same basis as others.
  • Capacity and load: what your child has to work with on a given day, and what the school day uses up. See Capacity and load.
  • NCCD: a yearly count of students receiving adjustments. It affects the school's funding, and you can ask your child's level.
  • Cognitive and academic assessment: a psychologist's measure of how your child thinks and learns (for example the WISC-V and the WIAT-III). It shows strengths and the areas where tasks will cost your child more.
