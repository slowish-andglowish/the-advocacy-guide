Step 2, worked example a

Japanese, taught two ways

The same subject, the same content, the same syllabus outcomes. Only the design changes, and with it, what the subject requires of the child.

The content (from a Year 7 task sheet). Read and write hiragana, one of the two Japanese sound alphabets. Learn the meaning of eight kanji, the picture-based characters: mountain, river, fire, sun, person, tree, book, Japan. Learn question-and-answer phrases: name, age, phone number, where you live, hobbies.
The study materials listed. Class notebook, textbook, homemade vocabulary cards. The test is handwritten in pen, in class.
The child. Year 7. Autistic. A reading disorder affecting the link between sounds and written symbols. Dyspraxia, which affects motor coordination, including handwriting.

3

Removing barriers is not enough

If a teaching method is a barrier, taking it away leaves a gap. Something has to be added in its place. What is added is where inclusion actually happens.

Step 1. Remove the barrierTyping instead of handwriting. Recognising instead of recalling. Practising before performing. These are required. They stop the subject measuring the disability.
Step 2. Add something betterTeach through the child's strengths, so the subject is enjoyable and the effort goes into understanding the material, not into struggling with known difficulties. This is a starting point to try, then review against the child's progress and enjoyment.

Step 2 is the hardest step, and the one most left to the school's judgement. It is also where inclusion succeeds or fails.

4

What changes, and what does not

Stays the sameThe subject, the content, the syllabus outcomes, the teacher, the class.
ChangesHow each part is taught, and how the child shows what they have learned.
What it costs the schoolA set of picture cards for hiragana, a keyboard setting on the laptop, and a differently worded test.

This is differentiated teaching, the ordinary practice of designing a lesson so that more students can reach the same goal. It is not a separate programme. For this child, it decides whether the subject requires their strengths or their barriers, all year.

The design of the subject is where discrimination happens, and where it is fixed.

About the two designs. The content and study materials come from a Year 7 Japanese task sheet. The left column describes a typical delivery drawn from those study materials, not observed teaching. The right column is an illustration of differentiated design, not the only way to do it.

About the evidence. Measured scores are from standardised testing (WISC-V and WIAT-III) in October 2021, at age 8. The two working memory scores are subtests: holding pictures in mind (picture span, 95th) and holding spoken sequences in mind (digit span, 50th). Which abilities each design requires, and how heavily, is a judgement from the design, not a measurement. Picture-based teaching of Japanese script: in one small university study (27 students), extra tutorials using picture mnemonics raised the pass rate significantly, though the rise in average marks was not significant (Manalo, Trafford & Mizutani, JALT Journal, 2004, 26(1)). Two kinds of change are shown. Removing a barrier rests on the child's diagnosis and on the legal duty to adjust. Building on a strength is a reasonable starting point, not a prediction: there is little controlled evidence that matching a method to a profile improves learning, so the approach should be reviewed against the child's progress. The study above tested students generally, not students with strong visual memory. Effort spent on the format of a task rather than its content is what learning researchers call extraneous load; reducing it leaves more capacity for learning the material (Sweller, Cognitive Science, 1988, 12(2):257–285). Typing in place of handwriting: whether the NSW syllabus requires handwritten script at Year 7 has not been confirmed here. Where it does, handwriting can be assessed separately, with adjustments.
