Step 4

Behaviour, bullying and discipline

When a neurodivergent child is in trouble at school, the behaviour is usually telling you something about the load. The law expects the school to listen.

1

Behaviour is a signal

Behaviour that comes from a disability is part of the disability under the law. The school has to respond to the cause, not only the behaviour.

  • The Disability Discrimination Act says that a disability includes "behaviour that is a symptom or manifestation of the disability" (section 4). This was added in 2009, after a High Court case, Purvis v New South Wales (2003), had narrowed it.
  • Running from class, shutting down, hiding, refusing, lashing out: for a neurodivergent child these are often signs that the load has gone past their capacity. See Capacity and load.
  • The useful question is what was happening just before. Which demand, at what time, in which setting?
2

What works better than punishment

Approaches that change the setting and solve problems with the child work better than consequences alone.

  • School-wide positive behaviour support reduces suspensions and office referrals (Bradshaw, Mitchell and Leaf 2010, Journal of Positive Behavior Interventions).
  • Collaborative problem solving, where adults and the child work out the problem together, reduced challenging behaviour as much as parent training in a randomised trial (Ollendick et al. 2016, Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology).
  • A punishment does not teach a child to manage a load they cannot manage. Reducing the load does.

What to ask before any discipline: Was this behaviour related to the disability? What adjustments were in place at the time? What will change so it does not happen again?

3

Bullying and exclusion

Nearly half of autistic children are bullied. Report it in writing. The school must act, and discipline must take the disability into account.

  • Bullying is common. Across 17 studies, 44% of autistic students were bullied at school (Maïano et al. 2016).
  • Signs include reluctance, avoiding certain places or times, and not wanting to talk about school.
  • Report each incident in writing, with the date. Ask what the school did, and ask for it to be recorded.
  • The school must have strategies to prevent disability harassment. There is no hardship exception to this duty.
  • Before any discipline, the school must ask whether the behaviour relates to the disability and whether adjustments were in place. Being asked to keep your child home "until it's sorted" is a suspension without the paperwork. Ask for it in writing.
