Step 7

The Advocate's Role

Know your child: what is hard, what helps, and how overload shows. Know their rights. Work with the school, and keep a record. And look after your own capacity while you do it.

1

Three steps

The legal duty is the school's. Your advocacy helps it do that duty well.

StepWhat it means
1. Know the dutyIt belongs to the school, and the school can't pass it to you. Once it knows about your child's disability, it must consult you and your child and make reasonable adjustments, without waiting for you to ask.
2. Say what you seeTell the school what is hard for your child and what helps, in writing. Your observations and your child's own words count. If you have clinicians, their letters can add weight.
3. Keep a recordKeep copies of what you sent and what the school replied, with dates. If things go wrong, you can ask for the school's own records under the Privacy Act.

Before you answer any school communication about any one thing, ask:

  • Is there a written plan, and do I have the current copy? If not, that is my first request.
  • Could this be related to my child's disability? Assume yes until shown otherwise.
  • What adjustment should have been in place for this situation? Was it?
  • Does the school's communication mention the disability or the plan? If not, my reply will.
  • Was I consulted about the adjustments that apply here, and when were they last reviewed?
  • Who holds the plan, and are they copied?
  • What do I want on the record: a question, a request, or a correction?

Reply to the disability, not to the incident.

How to make requests the school acts on

What to doWhy it works
Name the adjustments in writing.A clear written request is hard to set aside.
Be specific: the need, the evidence, the exact change, and a date to reply by.The school can act on it straight away. A vague request is easy to put off.
One issue per email.Each request gets its own answer, and is easier to track.
Write when you are calm. If you are upset, draft it and send it the next day.A calm, clear message is easier to act on and harder to dismiss. The duty does not depend on your tone, but how quickly things move often does.
Stick to what happened and what you saw. Leave out guesses about why.Facts are hard to argue with. Guesses about motive invite a defensive reply.
Keep feelings and requests apart. "My child was distressed" is a fact. "You don't care" is an accusation.The fact supports your request. The accusation becomes the topic instead.
Acknowledge what is working.It shows you are being fair, which makes your requests more credible.
Use your child's own words, and the school's own policies and plan.Your child's experience is evidence. The school is held to what it has already committed to.
Ask questions in writing.Meetings move fast and are hard to remember. A written answer is one you can come back to.
After every meeting or call, send a short summary and ask the school to correct anything wrong.Ideally the school keeps notes and sends them to you. If it doesn't, your summary does that job. It quickly gets confusing, and a record lets you see over time what was agreed and what changed.
Take a support person to meetings.They can take notes and notice what you miss.
If the school still does not act, and you have the means, ask a clinician to write to the school naming the adjustments.It is one way to escalate. It is harder for a school to set aside, but it is not required.
2

Your capacity counts too

A plan that relies on you as the backup only works while you have the capacity to do it.

  • When a school plan has gaps, parents fill them: walking a child in, collecting them early, managing devices at night, writing the emails.
  • Parents of autistic children report higher stress than parents of other children, including children with other disabilities (Hayes and Watson 2013, Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders).
  • Say plainly what you can and cannot carry. A plan should be built on what the school will do, not on what you will absorb.
3

If it still doesn't change

If nothing changes, take these steps in order.

  1. Write to the teacher or the person who holds the plan.
  2. Use the school's complaints policy, then go to the principal.
  3. For disability discrimination, contact the Australian Human Rights Commission, within 24 months.
  4. A disability advocacy service can help at any step.
  5. The school cannot treat you or your child worse for raising your rights or complaining. That is unlawful too.
  6. Private schools are exempt under NSW law but not under the federal Act and Standards. Complaints go to the Australian Human Rights Commission.

Words worth knowing

  • Reasonable adjustment: a change the school makes so your child can learn on the same basis as others.
  • Load: the total demand of the school day on your child (sensory, social, masking, organising, reading and writing, emotional), set against the capacity they have that day.
  • NCCD: a yearly count of students receiving adjustments. It affects the school's funding, and you can ask your child's level.
  • Cognitive and academic assessment: a psychologist's measure of how your child thinks and learns (for example the WISC-V for cognition and the WIAT-III for academic skills). It shows strengths, gaps, processing speed, working memory and literacy, and it reveals a weakness that high ability elsewhere can hide. It is the reference point for every adjustment and the evidence the school is obliged to act on.

The key laws

  • Disability Discrimination Act 1992: disability discrimination in education is unlawful, at every school.
  • Disability Standards for Education 2005: what the school must do to consult, adjust, review and prevent harassment.
  • Education Act 1990 (NSW): attendance, and the rules independent schools must meet to stay registered.
  • NSW Child Safe Standards: children take part in decisions that affect them.
  • Making a discrimination complaint (Legal Aid NSW): time limits and where to go.
