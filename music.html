Step 2, worked example b

What one music mark measured

A mark can show what your child knows, or only what the task required. This worked example sets one music task beside a child's assessment profile. It shows which of the child's abilities the task required, and just as importantly, what nobody knows.

The task, part 1: performing (out of 10). Sing a known song by note name (do, re, mi), and at the same time point to where each note sits on a music staff, using the fingers of one hand as the five lines. Keep a steady beat. Performed in class.
The task, part 2: listening (out of 10). The teacher plays rhythms and the child writes them down in a simple rhythm notation, at the teacher's pace. The teacher plays pitches and the child chooses the matching pattern from written music.
The child. Year 5. ADHD. A reading disorder affecting reading speed.
Result: Developing, 8 out of 20

What the overlay shows

Strengths the task usedThe child's ear (observed) and memory for the sequence (75th). The musical parts of the task sit here.
What the mark most likely reflectsThe barrier: doing several things at once at speed, and writing at someone else's pace (10th); performing live and watched (ADHD). None of this is music.
What nobody knowsTested musical aptitude, reading of music notation, and hand speed. The school did not have this evidence either.

So 8 out of 20 cannot tell anyone how musical this child is. It mostly records how hard it is for them to do several things at once, at someone else's speed, in front of others. Doing the task at all, against that barrier, took real effort.

3

How the harm compounds

  1. The task requires skills that, given the child's disability and profile, act as a barrier
  2. The mark records the barrier
  3. The mark is reported as "music"
  4. The child reads it as a measure of themselves: "I'm no good at music"

The first harm: the mark carries no usable information about what the child learnt in music. The task mixed the music with the barrier, so nobody can tell how much music they actually know: not the teacher, not the parent, not the child.

The second, often larger harm: the child believes the mark is a measure of them. In this case, the child did. They told a parent, "I'm no good at music."

Children's beliefs about their own ability and their results tend to reinforce each other over time, so a mistaken belief formed in Year 5 can shape what they attempt for years.

Marsh, H. W. & Craven, R. G. (2006). Reciprocal effects of self-concept and performance from a multidimensional perspective. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 1(2), 133–163.

This is normal teaching, before any disability law applies. The NSW Department of Education's guidance on differentiating learning says assessment "should be designed to maximise student opportunity to demonstrate what they have come to know, understand and be able to do." For a child with a disability, the Disability Standards for Education also make it a legal duty.

About the task. From the school's task sheet (Year 5 Music, Semester 1 2026). The report gives one result for both parts; how the 8 marks split between performing and listening is not known.

About the evidence. Measured scores are from standardised testing (WISC-V and WIAT-III) at ages 6 and 7, before ADHD medication. The pattern is the best evidence held; the levels may have changed since, particularly processing speed. "Hears pitch and rhythm" is a parent's observation. Which abilities the task requires, and how heavily, is a judgement from the design of the task, not a measurement. None of the tests listed measure musical ability.
